## 1. Channel types at a glance

| Type | Default style | Pacing | Voice tone | Default length |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Explainer | Editorial Motion Graphics (or Stickman Cartoon) | fast, rapid cuts, 5 cuts per block | casual second person, deadpan, hook + promise | 1 min (6 blocks) |
| History | Editorial Motion Graphics; alternates Paper Diorama, Mannequin | slower, chronological | witty, sarcastic, anachronistic storyteller (Mannequin: dry British) | 1 to 3 min (Mannequin about 90 s) |
| Kids | Studio 3D | FAST, 4 cuts per block, order varied | warm teacher, direct address, catchphrases, excited | 1 to 3 min |
| Picture Story | Flat 2D Papercraft | a new picture about every 1 s | tone of the chosen channel | 1 to 2 min |
| Fairy Tale and Myth | Cinematic Storybook (on twos) | slower, atmospheric, 5 cuts per block | enchanting storyteller, hushed, mysterious, no jokes | 2 to 3 min (12 to 18 blocks) |
| Long-form History | Watercolor Chronicle | documentary | measured documentary narrator | 10 / 15 / 20 min |

N = duration seconds / 10 blocks for every clip-based type. Duration is fixed: never shorten the video to fit short audio.

## 2. Kids

### The Kids EXPLAINER skeleton: THE QUESTION COMES FIRST

Almost every Kids topic is "what is X", "how does X work" or "why does X". A Kids video that opens on atmosphere reads as random to a child. The skeleton is fixed:

- **Block 1 = the question out loud, then the answer in the same breath.** Name the thing in the FIRST sentence, phrased the way a child would ask it, and answer it in plain words: *"What is a cell? A cell is a tiny room, and every living thing is built out of them."* No windup, no "today we are going to learn about", no scene-setting before the answer lands.
- **Block 2 = ONE concrete example the child can see.** Not "cells are everywhere" but "this leaf is packed with millions of them, stacked like bricks": a single named thing, doing the thing.
- **Middle blocks = one new idea each, in the order a child actually asks:** what is it -> where is it -> what does it do -> what happens without it. Never two ideas in one block.
- **Last block = the WOW then the CALLBACK.** One surprising quantity, said in words, then a closing line that answers the opening question again in four or five words ("so: a tiny room, everywhere").
- **THE TEST, run before approving the script:** read ONLY the first sentence of every block, in order. If those sentences alone do not answer the opening question, the script is random and gets rewritten, not patched with livelier wording.

### Kids plain language: the sentence a six-year-old repeats back

Density is not difficulty: a Kids line can be full and still easy.
- **One idea per sentence, at most two sentences per block.** A second clause hanging off a comma is a second idea; split it or drop it.
- **Everyday words only.** Say what a thing DOES before what it is CALLED, and name it once: "tiny rooms that living things are built from - cells" beats "cells are the basic structural unit of organisms". Abstract nouns (structure, process, function, system, energy) get replaced by something visible.
- **Compare to what a child already holds:** a grain of rice, a football pitch, a school bus, a bathtub, a heartbeat. Never a micron, a percentage or a scientific scale.
- **Small counts, said in words:** "three", "a hundred", "more than all the people in your town". No decimals, no ranges, no dates.
- **No stacked adjectives, no similes inside similes**, and no sentence that needs the previous sentence to make sense: a child who looks up mid-video must still land on their feet.
- **Read it out loud as a test:** if you run out of breath, or you would not say it to a six-year-old at a kitchen table, rewrite it. This test outranks hitting the top of the word band.

### Pictures must TELL the story, not decorate the narration

Every block shows a CHANGE, never a tableau:
- **One visible action per block, with a before and an after** (the lid comes off, the seed cracks, the water climbs the stem, the balloon leaves the hand). The 4-cut pattern stages that one action; it does not show four angles of the same standing pose.
- **The through-line prop is physically present AND physically different by the end:** it moves, opens, fills, multiplies, changes colour. A prop that only sits there is set decoration.
- **Forbidden:** a character standing and gesturing at an unchanged scene, "atmosphere" establishing beats with nothing happening, the same staging twice in one video.
- **The verb test:** if a block's shot text can be written without a verb, it is decoration; rewrite it with something happening.

Narrator-only is the DEFAULT Kids video. Talking characters and the sung song are opt-in sub-modes, not a fix for a weak script.

### The Kids interplay (narrator, character, viewer)

- **Characters react to the narrator on screen.** When the VO greets or asks ("Say hi to Masha!"), that block's SHOT beat stages the reaction: the character turns to camera and WAVES, nods, gasps, claps, points, presses a finger to lips. Write the reaction INTO the shot text, timed to the line's beat. Mouths never move with speech; reactions are gesture and face only.
- **Direct address, both ways.** The narrator names characters and talks to them ("Look how brave Masha is!") AND to the viewer ("Can YOU spot the red balloon?"). Characters look INTO the camera on viewer-addressed beats.
- **Call-and-response across block boundaries.** The question sits at the END of a block's line ("...can you count the apples?"); the block boundary IS the answer beat, and the NEXT block opens with the payoff ("That's right, three!"). Never leave a long pause inside a line for the answer.
- **Rich narration, always moving:** catchphrases, sound-words ("whoosh!", "ding!"), micro-celebrations ("hooray!") packed into the same 17 to 21-word flowing lines; warmth = word choice, never pauses.

### Kids voice pace

Lines run **17 to 21 words per 10 s block**, faster and more excited. The delivery phrase names the emotion outright (e.g. `warm thrilled storyteller, quick bouncy pace, bright smiling timbre, bursting with delight`); generous performed brackets ([giggles], [gasp], [mock surprise], [laughs]) are welcome, budgeted about 1 s each, and emotion words live IN the lines ("wow!", "amazing!", "here comes the best part!"). LINE HYGIENE (`vo-and-captions.md`) is enforced on Kids too: ONE exclamation and at most ONE sound-word or diminutive per line, no modifier repeated inside a line, every line teaches ONE new concrete thing. A Kids line that is only enthusiasm is a failed line.

## 3. Kids Song mode (a sung music video)

A Kids-only sub-direction: instead of a narrated explainer, a MUSIC VIDEO built on a real SUNG children's song (verses + repeating chorus). The song is made FIRST; the video blocks are staged TO the song; the one continuous song track lies over the whole cut. Triggered by "kids song", "nursery song video", "sing-along", "piosenka dla dzieci", "teledysk dla dzieci". Kids looks only.

Changes vs a normal Kids run: no narrator (the song IS the voice), no separate music bed, no captions (STT on singing is unreliable), clip SFX barely there (about 0.06). Everything else holds: style key, roster, 4-cut blocks, interplay staging, no lip-sync (characters dance, clap, jump, spin ON THE BEAT; humming smiles are fine, articulated singing mouths are not).

Pipeline: write the song (lyrics in the user's language, themed on the topic, starring the main character) -> generate the SONG (a song-generating model, prompt-only; the singing voice is described inside the prompt); check its duration is within +/-3 s of the target (60 or 120 s), max 3 attempts -> style key + roster (the main character, friends the lyrics name, 2 to 3 locations) -> block plan mapped to the song timeline:

- 2 min / 12 blocks: B1 intro + verse 1 opens; B2 verse 1; B3-B4 CHORUS 1; B5-B6 verse 2; B7-B8 CHORUS 2; B9-B10 verse 3; B11-B12 FINAL CHORUS (fullest).
- 1 min / 6 blocks: B1 intro + verse; B2-B3 chorus; B4 verse; B5-B6 final chorus.
- Verse blocks = story beats (show what the lyrics name). Chorus blocks = the SIGNATURE DANCE, repeated: same location, same recognizable staging every chorus, escalating (more friends, more props, fuller frame). The repetition IS the hook.
- Block prompts carry the lyric's meaning as choreography + "characters dance to an upbeat children's song, moving on a steady beat"; AUDIO line stays diegetic-only (claps, hops).

The SONG PROMPT template (2048 characters HARD LIMIT; count before sending). Two moods: **GENTLE** (lullaby-ish walks, nature, bedtime): C major, **100 BPM**, soft 4/4; ukulele + acoustic guitar, glockenspiel, gentle bass, soft flute, light chorus handclaps. **DANCE-ALONG** (party, exercise, action): G major, **112 BPM**, claps on every second beat, bouncy plucked ukulele + bass locked to the kick, marimba + sparkle bells; chorus packed with ACTION WORDS (clap, stomp, jump, spin).

> A full {1|2}-minute children's {song|dance-along song}, SUNG by a warm bright {gentle|cheerful, energetic} female singer with a smile in her voice - a real melodic song with verses and a repeating chorus on a clear singable tune, not narration. THE BEAT IS STEADY AND CONSTANT from the first second to the last: {100|112} BPM, simple 4/4, NO tempo changes, NO slowdowns, NO pauses between sections. Every lyric line has 8 syllables in steady trochaic meter (stress on 1,3,5,7), sung one syllable per beat, and EVERY line starts exactly on beat one of its bar; each line is one 4-bar phrase. Melody: simple {sweet|bouncy} nursery tune in {C|G} major, small steps within one octave, verse lower and playful, chorus a little higher and brighter. Instruments: {mood set from above}; clean warm mix, voice clearly in front, everything locked to the same steady beat. Structure with NO gaps: 4s instrumental intro, Verse 1, Chorus, Verse 2, Chorus, Verse 3, Chorus (final, fuller, extra claps and bells), 4s outro on {bells + a happy character sound}.
> Verse 1: "{4 lines, 8 syllables each}" · Chorus: "{4 lines, catchy, action words, repeats verbatim}" · Verse 2 · Chorus: (repeat) · Verse 3 · Chorus: (repeat, final, fuller)
> Delivery: {soft and melodic | energetic but sweet}, perfectly in tune, every syllable landing right on the steady beat; innocent and age-appropriate for toddlers. Total length approximately {1|2} minutes.

Check the prompt BEFORE submitting (each retry costs a full generation): steady-beat clause present; no tempo-change words (bridge, slow down, speed up, ritardando); a tested BPM (100 or 112); every lyric line 8 syllables; chorus identical each time; no groove jargon ("four-on-the-floor", "syncopation", "drop"); at most 2048 characters. Laws: ONE constant tempo (dynamics come only from orchestration: the final chorus gets fuller); chorus text repeats VERBATIM; lyrics concrete, visual, age-appropriate, main character named; 1-minute songs = 2 verses + 2 choruses; trim instrument adjectives first, never the meter laws or lyrics.

## 4. Kids Talking Characters

Only when the user wants characters that speak. Storytelling ALTERNATES: **odd blocks = NARRATION** (external narrator, mouths closed) and **even blocks = DIALOGUE** (characters speak their own lines, lips and all, from a video model that generates speech with the clip). Block count is free; the alternation runs across however many blocks exist.

- **ONE story, chronologically bound.** Every dialogue block is CAUSED by the narration block before it and sets up the one after (narrator says the bee's tummy hurts -> the bee says it out loud and the butterfly promises a doctor -> narrator flies the butterfly across the meadow -> the butterfly reaches the doctor). A dialogue block that could be swapped with another is broken; no character may know something the story has not shown yet.
- **Word budgets differ by block kind:** narration keeps the Kids band (17 to 21 words per 10 s); dialogue blocks are **16 to 22 words total across the block** (two speakers need turn-taking gaps; at 16 to 26 the gaps became dead air, so the floor went up). Kid dialogue lands harder spare: "Ohhh, my tummy hurts so much!" / "Don't worry, I'll find a doctor, fast!"
- **At most TWO speaking characters per dialogue block**, named in the block, each with ONE turn (two at most).
- **Voices come from the video model itself:** a character's timbre MAY drift between dialogue blocks; keep every other identity anchor rock solid (same design, same palette).
- **Audio per block:** narration blocks get a TTS take as usual; dialogue blocks use the CLIP'S OWN audio, extracted at full length, normalized to -16 LUFS, never re-centred, never time-stretched, so the lips stay locked. Captions then read each block's own voice file.
- Not compatible with Song mode inside one video. Everything else is normal Kids.

## 5. Picture Story (narrated stills, frame-by-frame)

A MOTION MODE that works with every channel type and every look. The video is built from STILL IMAGES: a narrated storybook where each picture stays on screen exactly as long as its line takes to say (timing comes from the audio, not from 10 s windows). Triggered by "picture story", "stills", "slideshow", "storybook video", "one image per line", "film ze zdjęć", "bajka obrazkowa". Default look Flat 2D Papercraft (`style-packs.md` section 8).

**FRAME-BY-FRAME, not a slideshow.** The audio is ONE continuous narration (never 2 to 3 s snippets). Word timestamps from speech-to-text give a timeline and FRAMES are laid onto it. A single moment gets a SMALL BURST of near-identical frames that each change ONE detail, so it reads as movement:

> "woke up, on his back" -> F1 WIDE: John flat on his back, eyes closed. (same shot) -> F2: eyes OPEN. (same shot) -> F3: head turned, squinting. "his face - a scowl" -> F4 CLOSE-UP: John's face neutral. (same shot) -> F5: brows knit, scowl lands. "he sat up on the bed" -> F6 MEDIUM: sitting up, mid-rise. "shuffled down the hall" -> F7: walking the hallway. (same shot) -> F8: still walking, scratching his head. "brushing his teeth" -> F9 INTERIOR: brush AT his mouth. (same shot) -> F10: hand DOWN, done, foam on lip.

- A new SHOT (new framing) whenever the line changes action, place or subject; WITHIN a shot, 2 to 3 micro-variation frames carry the little movement.
- **Shot mix:** each frame names WIDE / MEDIUM / CLOSE-UP, mixed RANDOMLY with one hard ban: two CLOSE-UPs never run back to back (a healthy run reads `W M W C W W M C W`). CLOSE-UP then MEDIUM is the money transition.
- **Cadence:** a frame every about 0.7 to 1.2 s; no frame holds longer than 1.5 s. A 1-minute story = **45 to 70 frames** (never fewer than about 40); 2 minutes = 90 to 140. A floor of `ceil(narration seconds / 1.5)` frames; plan the count before generating.
- **Two frame kinds.** NEW-FRAMING frame: a fresh render composed from the assets (location, character sheets, props as references), used only when the action or place changes (the minority). **EDIT frame (about half of all frames, never more than two in a row):** the PREVIOUS rendered frame is the ONE and ONLY reference (no asset sheets, no location, no props: adding them makes the model rebuild the scene). Prompt:
  > "Take the reference image and keep it EXACTLY: same composition, same crop, same camera, same character, same colors, same background, same style. Change ONLY: {one small detail - eyes open / brows knit / hand lowers / mouth opens / foam appears}. Do not redraw or re-stage anything else."
  Rhythm: one new framing, then one or two edits, then the next new framing. Never more than two edits in a row, never more than two new framings in a row. The chain is capped at 2 edits (about 3 seconds); longer chains read as one picture with glitches. **The one change must be visible at a glance** (a hand moves, the head turns, the mouth opens, an object enters or leaves); a single eyebrow tweak is invisible in one second.
- **Frame duration is arithmetic:** `duration(n) = start(n+1) - start(n)` with starts straight from the speech-to-text timestamps; the last frame runs to the end of the audio. Guessed or evenly spread durations make pictures drift ahead of the words. Spot-check frames at 25 / 50 / 75 percent: the frame at that timestamp must show what is being said then. Before assembly, print the ledger as `NNN -> the beat's first five words` and read it top to bottom to catch frames out of story order.
- Every frame uses the chosen aspect (never square/2:3 in the cut), no in-frame text, 1080p-class is enough; asset sheets are references only and never appear in the cut.
- Voice: ONE continuous narration (chunks of whole paragraphs, about 1800 characters, same voice and delivery phrase, joined losslessly). The Kids bed rule and music rules as in `vo-and-captions.md`; no clip SFX in this mode.
- Does NOT apply here: 10 s windows, 3/4-cut templates, {MOTION} tokens, freeze checks, impact beats. Everything else (voice lock, palette lock, no on-screen text) applies.

## 6. Long-form History documentary (10+ minutes)

A full YouTube-length documentary: one era, one campaign, one reign told end to end. **Minimum 10 minutes (N >= 60 blocks); offer 10 / 15 / 20 min.** Default look: Watercolor Chronicle (alternates Paper Diorama, Editorial Motion Graphics). Auto-lock this format when the request says 10 minutes or more and/or "documentary", "dokument".

Warn BEFORE starting, in plain words: a 10-minute run is 60+ clips + 60+ voice takes + a large asset roster, so generation takes a long time (often hours) and a proportionally large generation budget. This is the heaviest format; if planning falls apart or gates keep failing, say so rather than grinding on.

The documentary skeleton (this is the product):
1. **COLD OPEN, in medias res (blocks 1 to 2):** drop the viewer into the most dramatic minute, mid-crisis, no setup ("He rode toward the gunfire expecting victory; he found his own army streaming back").
2. **THE REWIND (block 3):** one explicit pivot line ("to understand how he ended up on that road, you have to go back six months"), then chronology.
3. **CHAPTERS (the body):** 5 to 8 chronological chapters of 8 to 14 blocks (about 1.5 to 2.5 min). Each has its OWN mini-arc (setup -> pressure -> turn) and its own through-line beat; chapters end on a hook that hands over to the next ("That answer was coming faster than anyone expected").
4. **HUMAN VIGNETTE (at least 1 per chapter):** one zoomed-in human story (the drillmaster swearing in three languages, the water carrier at the guns), concrete, named where history names them, honest where it does not.
5. **HONEST UNCERTAINTY:** where historians disagree, SAY SO in one line ("what happened next has been debated for two hundred years"). Non-negotiable.
6. **THE MEANING (last chapter):** why it mattered, the before/after contrast stated plainly ("the army that left was not the army that arrived").
7. **OUTRO + CTA (final block):** the ONE place the narrator may address the channel (subscribe / comment the next topic / like). One block, warm and brief; characters never gesture at UI elements.

Two-stage script lock: first the CHAPTER OUTLINE (chapters + eras + cast/ERA MAP + through-line + vignette list), then the full block script. Research is mandatory and deeper: per chapter 4 to 6 verified concretes, cross-checked, Sources kept. Generate chapter by chapter. The through-line spans the whole film, escalating per chapter, resolved in the meaning chapter. A quiet music bed is wanted; the voice is a measured but warm documentary narrator, one locked voice for all 60+ takes.

**ERA MAP (the asset math, before any generation):** long history spans YEARS; people age, buildings burn, a single character sheet WILL drift.
1. Split the story into ERAS aligned with chapters (`1509-1515 young king`, `1530s break with Rome`, `1540s old age`).
2. For every recurring character list the eras they appear in and WHAT CHANGES (age, build, hair, dress, status markers). **One character asset PER ERA.** Identity chain: the FIRST incarnation is generated from the style key; every LATER variant is generated WITH the first incarnation attached and framed "the SAME person as in the reference, now {era changes}" (for 3+ eras also attach the immediately previous era). Identity invariants are stated verbatim in every variant's prompt.
3. Name variants explicitly (`henry_young`, `henry_old`, `anne_1530s`) and in every block use ONLY the variant matching that block's era.
4. Locations get era variants too when the story changes them (the palace before/after the fire). Otherwise reuse, budgeting about 1 to 2 locations + coverage per chapter.
5. Sum the roster BEFORE generating: characters x eras + locations + coverage + props (through-line included). A 10-minute run lands at 25 to 40 assets; show the count and the map at outline stage. Mannequin collapses the map to child/adult per character.

**Visual variety law (anti-repetition):** sixty blocks of the same three gestures read as a screensaver.
- SHOW WHAT THE LINE NAMES (the line says spices, spices are on screen, not another wax seal).
- MOTIF BUDGET: keep a motif ledger; the same recurring action beat (stamp slams, seal presses, signings, troop marches) appears at most ONCE per chapter and about 3 times per film (through-line beats exempt, each showing a NEW state). Two blocks never share the same action beat back to back.
- MANDATORY interleaves for History: every chapter has at least ONE map beat (routes drawing, regions filling, pins pulsing) and at least ONE quantity beat whenever the VO cites money, resources, armies or years, shown as visual comparison (coin stacks growing, ship rows multiplying, a granary emptying), numerals banned on screen.
- Rotate the device class between blocks: scene with characters -> map -> object/quantity -> scene -> vignette; never three of the same class in a row.

## 7. Fairy Tale and Myth (what changes)

It is a look + tone + music profile on the standard clip pipeline, not a new mechanic: Cinematic Storybook look (`style-packs.md` section 6), on-twos cadence (Posterize Time 12 fps in the edit), hushed no-jokes storyteller voice, mandatory mysterious-calm music bed, optional book-spread inserts, 2 to 3 minute default (12 to 18 blocks), five cuts per block. Avoid grim-death vocabulary in prompts (filters); convey dark mood through architecture, light and nature. Topics: skip factual research, invent freely, nothing fake about the real world; Slavic and Polish folklore (Baba Jaga, Smok Wawelski, Złota Kaczka, Świteź) fit the look well.
